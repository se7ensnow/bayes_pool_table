Bayes’ theorem, played out on a pool table

A white ball is somewhere on the table and you cannot see it. Red balls land around it, and all you note is which side each one fell on. That one bit of information is enough to narrow down where the white ball must be.

Try the table

1The central idea: learning from evidence

Imagine a white ball resting somewhere on a pool table. Its exact vertical position is unknown to us.

We want to estimate where it is, but we cannot observe it directly. Instead, we throw several red balls onto the table and watch where they land relative to the white ball: below it, or above it.

The important observation is that the proportion of red balls landing below the white ball gives us information about its position.

If most red balls land below it, the white ball is probably located relatively high on the table. If most land above it, the white ball is probably low.

This is the intuition behind Bayesian inference: using observations to improve our knowledge about something we cannot see.

2Starting with an initial belief: the prior

Before throwing any red balls, we have no information about the white ball’s position. So we start with an initial assumption: all possible positions are equally likely.

Let’s call the unknown position of the white ball R. For the sake of simplicity, we use a discretized set of positions:

R ∈ {r1, r2, …, rm},  rj ∈ [0, 1] (1)

where each rj represents a possible height, with the total height of the pool table set to 1.

If all positions are equally likely, the probability of the white ball being at any particular position is:

P(R = rj) = 1m (2)

For example, with 100 possible positions, each initially has a probability of 0.01.

This initial probability distribution is called the prior. It represents what we believe before taking new observations into account.

3Observations provide evidence: the likelihood

We now throw red balls and observe their positions. For simplicity, let’s assign a value to each observation:

  • Xi = 1: the i-th red ball lands below the white ball.
  • Xi = 0: the i-th red ball lands above the white ball.

Suppose the white ball is at height r. The probability of a red ball landing below it is:

P(Xi = 1 ∣ R = r) = r (3)

And the probability of landing above it is:

P(Xi = 0 ∣ R = r) = 1 − r (4)

This makes intuitive sense: the higher the white ball is, the more likely a randomly placed red ball is to land below it.

Now imagine throwing n red balls. If k of them land below the white ball, the remaining n − k land above it. Assuming the throws are independent, the probability of observing this particular sequence is:

P(B ∣ R = r) = n∏i = 1 P(Xi = xi ∣ R = r) = rk(1 − r)n−k (5)

Here B is the set of observations we made, r is a possible position of the white ball, k is the number of red balls below it and n − k the number above it.

This probability is called the likelihood. It tells us how compatible our observations are with each possible position of the white ball: a higher likelihood means a position provides a better explanation for what we observed.

4Updating our beliefs: Bayes’ theorem

We now have two important pieces of information: our initial belief about the white ball’s position (the prior), and how well each possible position explains our observations (the likelihood).

Bayes’ theorem combines them to produce an updated probability distribution, called the posterior. The general equation is:

P(A ∣ B) = P(B ∣ A) P(A)P(B) (6)

In our experiment, we can translate it into:

P(R = r ∣ B) = P(B ∣ R = r) P(R = r)P(B) (7)
P(R = r ∣ B)
the posterior — the probability that the white ball is at position r, given our observations.
P(B ∣ R = r)
the likelihood — the probability of obtaining our observations if the white ball is at position r.
P(R = r)
the prior — the initial probability of that position.
P(B)
the evidence — the overall probability of obtaining our observations, considering all possible positions.

The evidence normalizes the result: it ensures that the probabilities of all possible positions add up to 1. It is calculated by considering every possible position, using the law of total probability and equation (5) for the likelihood:

P(B) = m∑j = 1 P(B ∣ R = rj) P(R = rj) = 1m m∑j = 1 rjk(1 − rj)n−k (8)

The final result is therefore:

P(R = rj ∣ B) = rjk(1 − rj)n−km∑l = 1rlk(1 − rl)n−k (9)

This equation gives us the updated probability of the white ball occupying each possible position.

5A numerical example

Let’s consider a simple experiment. Suppose the white ball can be located at one of a hundred possible heights, spaced 0.01 apart:

R ∈ {0.01, 0.02, 0.03, …, 1.00}

Before throwing any red balls, all hundred positions are equally likely, so the prior probability of each position is

P(R = rj) = 1100 = 0.01

Now we throw n = 10 red balls and observe k = 7 below the white ball and n − k = 3 above it. The likelihood of any possible position r becomes

P(B ∣ R = r) = r7(1 − r)3

Let’s compare two of the hundred positions:

Position A, r = 0.3 P(B ∣ R = 0.3) = 0.37 · 0.73 = 0.0000750141
Position B, r = 0.7 P(B ∣ R = 0.7) = 0.77 · 0.33 = 0.0022235661

The second likelihood is about 29.64 times higher than the first. This means that observing seven red balls below and three above is much more compatible with the white ball being at height 0.7 than at height 0.3.

However, likelihood alone does not give us the final probability. We must also consider the prior and normalize across all possible positions. After doing this, we obtain the posterior distribution — and you can watch it happen below.

For example, the position r = 0.7 has a likelihood of 0.0022235661, while the likelihoods of all hundred positions add up to 0.0757575841. Dividing the first number by the second gives the posterior probability of this position:

P(R = 0.7 ∣ B) = 0.00222356610.0757575841 ≈ 0.02935

This is the height of the bar at r = 0.7. Before throwing the red balls, this position had a probability of 0.01; now it has 0.02935, about three times as much. Repeating the same calculation for the other positions gives the remaining bars, and all hundred probabilities add up to 1.

The most probable position is now r = 0.7. However, this does not mean that we know exactly where the white ball is. Other positions are still possible. We have simply used our observations to improve our initial belief about the position of the white ball.

6The process is iterative

One particularly interesting aspect of Bayesian inference is that the process can be repeated. Imagine throwing another ten red balls after the first ten.

We can now use our previous posterior as our new prior, incorporate the additional observations, and calculate an updated posterior.

Prior1 new evidence Posterior1 more evidence Posterior2

In the lab above, keep adding balls and watch the bars concentrate on fewer heights. As more evidence becomes available, our understanding of the white ball’s position generally becomes more precise — looking at the second direction of the pool table, left to right, would sharpen it further still.

We are progressively reducing our uncertainty about something we cannot directly observe.

7The bigger picture

The pool table experiment illustrates a much broader principle. Bayes’ theorem is about how to reason under uncertainty and how to update our beliefs when new information becomes available.

We start with what we believe, observe what happens, evaluate how well our observations fit our assumptions, and update what we believe accordingly.

That is the fundamental idea behind Bayes’ theorem.
